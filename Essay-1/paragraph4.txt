Taking the lessons learned by previous generations into account, artists of this generation are utilizing tools and technologies not previously available to circumvent the claws of predatory contracts and label overreach to control their own distribution and release music on their own schedules rather than waiting for approval from boardrooms. This is possible in large part due to the change in the nature of distribution over the last 20 years, with the horizon of digital stores like iTunes, and later their streaming counterparts in Apple Music, and Spotify. The shift in the primary mode of the consumption of music from physicals (CDs, vinyls, cassettes, etc.) to digital mp3 files, has caused a market boom in the demand for digital aggregator and distribution platforms, and companies like DistroKid, CDBaby, Tunecore, and United Masters have quickly filled that space capturing a massive market share. Aggregators have changed the game for accessibility, as artists that may have never previously been signed to a label are able to release music on their own, and retain larger percentages of generated revenues than was possible in the label era (John and Ahern 4). This is particularly advantageous for artists that have long had to struggle to retain ownership, as this new pseudo direct to consumer model has allowed for “more flexibility…Artists are now more able to retain ownership rights, negotiate favorable contracts, and maintain creative autonomy” (Tate).
