While only one among the many tools in their repertoire, shelving projects was and remains common practice among major labels in the music industry used to police artists’ expression and ability to curate an image or brand in line with their voice and beliefs; typically in order to ensure profits and protect bottom lines. This tactic has injured the careers and slowed the momentum of many artists, however the nature of  the controversy surrounding Lupe Fiasco’s third studio album, Lasers, serves as an interesting case study among them. From his debut project in 2006, Food and Liquor, Lupe had already established himself as a politically conscious artist with songs like American Terrorist, touching on the War in Iraq, America’s crumbling education system, and allusions to class consciousness beyond racial divisions. Thus Lupe and fans were both naturally outraged when discovering that his label, Atlantic Records, would not release the album “because they feared it lacked the commercial singles that they had envisioned”(Martinez 23); however not necessarily surprising as it was not the first time they had interfered with his artistic process(Martinez 24). While the album eventually released in 2011, after a petition created by fans forced the labels hands (Mosweaty), continued battles with his label on the creative direction of his career damaged the potential reach and longevity of his career.
