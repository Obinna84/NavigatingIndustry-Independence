In addition to finding new ways to reclaim control over their voice, catalogue, and brand through distribution, independent artists of this generation are taking advantage of social media algorithms to curate audiences specific to them allowing them even more flexibility and control in how they craft their image. Now years removed from its inception, many studies have been done researching the phenomenon of “Black Twitter” and how its operation as a countervailing force to mainstream narratives about blackness, especially during times of racial strife following instances of police brutality (Trayvon Martin, Micahel Brown, Tamir Rice, etc.) allowed for the cultivation of online movements and greater self definition for black internet users (Lavan 56-57). The benefits of social media algorithms for marginalized communities extend beyond self definition and the production of social capital, but also include the consolidation of communities and social spaces built around shared beliefs and interests. This has been especially beneficial for artists as it makes finding audiences to market one’s music easier, and creates pathways to stability for artists that may deviate from the mainstream sound of their genres. Redveil, an independent rapper from Prince George’s county, Maryland, has spoken on the extent to which advertising his music on Twitter helped him get his start in music, stating, “As soon as the pandemic started, I put out this song called soul food. I put the music video out on Twitter, and that was the first thing that went up. It started buzzing on Twitter, and I would reply to people’s tweets and it would do well every time.”(Morton). Not only was this beneficial in jump starting his career, but building such an organic fanbase allowed him to have a solid foundation to openly express his support for Palestine following the Israeli assault on Gaza in late 2023 at Camp Flog Gnaw(“Camp Flog Gnaw”), that otherwise might’ve landed him in troubled waters had he been signed to a label.
