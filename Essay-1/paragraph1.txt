It no longer remains a secret shrouded in mystery that the music industry, for as long as it has existed, has taken advantage of, and exploited the very artists that it relies on for its preservation (John and Ahern 4), especially those who come from historically marginalized backgrounds. Artists have long spoken out about the predatory nature of label relationships and ownership contracts, but lacked the legal lexicon and capital to properly leverage these arguments in court to take back ownership (Rahman), and the technology necessary to evade barriers in their creative expression. However, in the era of digital distribution and streaming the tides appear to be shifting. More so than ever, artists are opting for independence (Data USA), choosing to navigate production, distribution, audience cultivation and interaction by themselves (or in trusted partnership with smaller labels and management circles), rather than relying on discovery by A&R scouts to sign heavily slanted contracts with major labels (John and Ahern 3). One of the largest factors in the development of this phenomenon has to do with the onset of technologies allowing for the increased accessibility of music production and distribution tools/software, as well as cultural shifts in music discovery that have been led by Digital Streaming Platforms (DSPs) and social media algorithms. While this new found freedom has created new avenues for artists to retain control and ownership of their catalogue, brand, and voice, it has also bred a wealth of problems that may be just as insidious as those that plagued prior generations.
