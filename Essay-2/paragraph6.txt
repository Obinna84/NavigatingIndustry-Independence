However, this is not to say that algorithms have become a net negative for the proliferation of creative expression. Rather just as strongly as shorter and formulaic content is pushed, more space has been opened up for the development and growth of niches and subgenres as a result of algorithmic personalization (UChicago Alumni & Friends). The “Explore” and “For You” pages of today’s social media platforms are molded by users, and the content displayed back to them becomes a reflection of previous interactions they’ve made with other posts. As a result artists that may not fit into the dominant sounds of their overall genre are given the chance to not only find an audience, but find one that is reflective of their brand, aesthetic, and sound. Redveil, a young artist from Prince George’s county, Maryland, known for his use of choppy samples, and vibrant and experimental production serves as a bright example of this occurrence. When speaking about using Twitter as the first place to advertise his music in an interview with XXL, a prominent hip hop magazine, he said, “as soon as the pandemic started, I put out this song called soul food. I put the music video out on Twitter, and that was the first thing that went up. It started buzzing on Twitter, and I would reply to people’s tweets and it would do well every time” (Morton). Building a fanbase organically like this would eventually result in him amassing 837,000 monthly listeners on Spotify at the time of writing, performing at some of the largest music festivals like Coachella, Rolling Loud, and Camp Flog Gnaw, and allowing him an undeniable flexibility in expression, seen most in vocalizing his support for Palestine at Camp Flog Gnaw in 2023 (“Camp Flog Gnaw”).
